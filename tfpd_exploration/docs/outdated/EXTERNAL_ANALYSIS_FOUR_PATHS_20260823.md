## Part 1 — Four paths

Path 1, Label-Budget-Matched Carrier Uncertainty. SPINT's Neural ID and your carrier are both point estimates. Replace the point estimate with a pair, the estimated carrier and its covariance, and feed both into the model. During training, inject identity noise whose strength matches the effective sample size you will actually have at deployment, together with the decoupled dropout; at test time, use the shrunk carrier. The deliverable is not a single number but a label-budget-to-performance calibration curve across 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 minutes, 4 minutes, and oracle, whose shape you can predict in advance from the analytic form of the carrier covariance, which scales as noise variance times the inverse Gram matrix of the behavior covariates divided by the effective sample size. Predicting the curve and then hitting it is a much stronger claim than reporting a small gain in R squared. Cost is moderate because it requires retraining. The risk is a false null, which needs the block bootstrap fix discussed earlier.

Path 2, Behavior Space as a Canonical Identity Frame. This is the deep implication of your first contribution and I do not think you have cashed it in yet. SPINT's Neural ID lives in a session-specific activity space and is only determined up to some unspecified invertible transform, so it is useful but not canonical. Your carrier lives in behavior covariate space: a preferred direction is a vector in velocity coordinates, independent of whose cortex, which array, and which region produced it. Three things follow. First, you can write a short proposition stating that context identity is identifiable only within a session, up to a session-dependent transform, whereas the behavior-space carrier is canonical, or gauge-fixed. That costs zero GPU hours and is exactly the kind of statement a NeurIPS-type reviewer rewards. Second, cross-subject, cross-array, and cross-region transfer should be possible for you and is structurally impossible for SPINT, which raises the magnitude of your claim rather than adding a module. Third, and this is a simplification rather than an addition, in a shared behavior coordinate system Hungarian matching is no longer necessary because identities are directly comparable, and being both stronger than the baseline and simpler by one component is a very powerful story. There is a feasibility gate you must clear before writing any code: you need at least two sessions or subjects that share a behavior covariate space. The FALCON datasets define their covariates differently and I am not confident that the H1, M1, and M2 velocity spaces can be aligned; if they cannot, fall back to cross-array, cross-region, or a multi-subject subset within a single dataset. This check is the go or no-go for the whole path and requires only reading the data documentation.

Path 3, Gradient-Free Within-Session Identity Tracking. SPINT infers identity once in the calibration window and then holds it static, while real sessions drift within themselves. Instead, use the decoder's own output as a pseudo-label and recursively update the same carrier estimator online, by recursive least squares or a Kalman filter over the carrier. This is closed-loop and requires no new labels, no gradients, and no retraining. Because it sits entirely inside the regime SPINT itself claims, namely no labels and no parameter updates at test time, you can compare against SPINT head to head. GPU cost is close to zero, which matters a lot given your current compute constraint. The risk is self-reinforcing pseudo-label drift, so you need a fail-closed trigger, for example freezing the update whenever the angular change in the carrier exceeds a threshold.

Path 4, Calibration-Cost-Normalized Evaluation Protocol. Your existing label ledger, the three-line oracle diagnostic, and the negative-result set can be packaged as a comparison protocol that normalizes by calibration cost, since existing work puts unlabeled few-shot and supervised few-shot methods in the same table without matching their label budgets. This belongs in the paper, but as a methodological contribution alone it is too light to carry the second novelty claim. Treat it as armor that raises the credibility of your main contribution, not as a second pillar.

On ordering, the three-line oracle diagnostic already selects among these. If oracle performance is close to your current best, the bottleneck is not carrier estimation, so the canonical-frame path is the only real upgrade and the uncertainty path will return a null. If oracle is far above and a four-minute scattered calibration matches oracle, the bottleneck is within-session drift, so promote the identity-tracking path to the main contribution and note that the uncertainty path is aimed at the wrong target. If oracle is far above and the scattered four-minute condition matches contiguous calibration, the bottleneck is estimation variance and the uncertainty path is exactly on target. So the sequence is: clear the canonical-frame feasibility gate by reading the data documentation, run the zero-GPU diagnostic, then pick the second contribution, and only then spend compute. Finally, do not manufacture a second novelty by bolting on an architectural widget such as a new attention variant, a contrastive auxiliary loss, or a self-supervised pretraining stage. Those are orthogonal to your first contribution, reviewers will correctly read them as filler, and they happen to be the most GPU-expensive options available, whereas the canonical-frame proposition, the test-time tracking method, and the evaluation protocol are all low-compute and high-information.

## Part 2 — High-dimensional labels (H1/M1/M2)

Before the list, two caveats. I do not have verbatim access to that earlier analysis, so what follows is a reconstruction from the same premises rather than a recall; if any item contradicts what we settled on before, trust the earlier version. And I checked one fact just now: H1's behavior is documented as 7 degrees of freedom, specifically 3 translation, 1 rotation (roll), and 3 grasp-shaping dimensions. For M1 I am not confident about the exact covariate definition, since the FALCON paper describes the movement tasks as predicting multi-dimensional covariates "such as muscle activity," which suggests at least one of the monkey datasets is EMG rather than kinematics. Whether your labels are position, velocity, both, or muscle activity, and what the nominal dimension is, changes the numbers in items one, two, and six, so correct me there.

Effective Behavior Dimension, Not Nominal Dimension. The first thing to fix is that seven is almost certainly not the number that matters. In a cued, trial-structured task the seven DoF are strongly co-varying, grasp-shaping dimensions move together and translation is structured by target layout, so the Gram matrix of the behavior covariates is ill-conditioned and the effective dimension, measured as the participation ratio of its eigenvalue spectrum, may be three or four. Every prediction you make about label budget should be written in terms of this effective dimension, not the nominal one. Computing it per session costs zero GPU hours and one afternoon, and it is a prerequisite for the rest of the list, because if effective dimension turns out to be close to two then H1 is not actually the high-dimensional showcase you think it is and several items below collapse.

Carrier Dimension Scaling of the Label Budget. Each unit's carrier is a vector with as many coefficients as behavior dimensions, so the number of quantities you must estimate per unit grows linearly with dimension while the labels available stay fixed, and the covariance scales as noise variance times the inverse Gram matrix divided by effective sample size. The direct consequence is that the label-budget-to-performance curve should sit further to the right for a high-dimensional dataset than for a two-dimensional one, by a predictable amount. This is the strongest cheap claim available to you, because it is a cross-dataset prediction from a single closed-form expression: the same theory that fits the low-dimensional curve should forecast where the high-dimensional curve saturates, before you run it. Reporting a forecast and then hitting it across datasets of differing dimension is worth more than any single-number improvement.

Anisotropic Carrier Noise and Behavior-Space Whitening. Because the Gram matrix is ill-conditioned, carrier estimation error is not isotropic: coefficients along well-explored behavior directions are precise and coefficients along weakly-explored directions are close to noise. A single scalar confidence per unit therefore misrepresents the situation, and so does any Euclidean distance or angle computed on raw carriers. Two corrections follow. Use shrinkage or ridge with the behavior covariance so that poorly-explored directions are pulled toward the population prior rather than trusted, and use the Mahalanobis metric induced by that covariance everywhere you currently use an angle, including the fail-closed trigger in the test-time tracking path, whose threshold is otherwise dominated by the noisiest direction. This is the high-dimensional version of the uncertainty path, and it is where that path earns its keep, since in two dimensions the anisotropy is small enough to ignore.

Identity Separability Grows with Behavior Dimension. This is the effect that runs opposite to the budget cost, and it is the reason H1 should be your headline dataset rather than your hard case. More behavior dimensions means each unit's carrier is a point in a larger space, so two units with genuinely different tuning are less likely to collide, and expected pairwise carrier separation grows faster than the per-coefficient estimation noise as long as effective dimension is not saturated. Meanwhile, the competing approach, which infers identity from activity structure alone, gets harder in high-dimensional tasks because activity covariance no longer factorizes into a small number of well-separated conditions. So the prediction is that your margin over the baseline widens with effective behavior dimension, and narrows toward zero on the two-dimensional datasets. That is a mechanism-level prediction with a sign, not a leaderboard number, and it is exactly the kind of claim that survives review even if one dataset underperforms.

Coverage-Optimal Calibration Snippet Selection. In high dimensions the conditioning problem gives you a free intervention that does not exist in low dimensions. A contiguous four-minute calibration block samples whatever DoF happened to be cued in that window, whereas a budget-matched set of shorter snippets chosen to cover all cue types makes the Gram matrix far better conditioned at identical label cost. This is classical experimental design, so you can select snippets by a D-optimality or condition-number criterion, and the deliverable is that a well-chosen two minutes beats a contiguous four minutes. That result is simultaneously a practical recommendation for iBCI calibration sessions and a clean confirmation that your bottleneck analysis is correct, and it requires no retraining. I think this is the item most likely to graduate from supporting evidence to an actual second contribution, because it is a logical consequence of the carrier estimator rather than an addition to it, and because it directly repairs the confound in the scattered-versus-contiguous comparison that the three-line diagnostic already needs.

Shared Behavior Subspace Embedding for Cross-Dataset Transfer. This resolves the feasibility gate I flagged on the canonical-frame path. A seven-dimensional space and a two-dimensional space are not the same frame, so naive cross-dataset transfer is ill-posed, but the translation dimensions are a genuinely shared physical subspace, and carriers can be projected onto it and compared across subjects and species. Transfer on the shared subspace, with the non-shared dimensions masked rather than zero-padded, is a much weaker requirement than full frame alignment and is still structurally impossible for a method whose identity lives in session-specific activity space. Be honest in the writeup that this is partial transfer, and be careful that masking is implemented as marginalization, not as substituting zeros, which would silently assert no tuning.

Per-Degree-of-Freedom Gain Attribution. The benchmark aggregates across dimensions, which hides the mechanism. Break every result out per DoF and predict in advance, from the eigenspectrum of the Gram matrix, which dimensions should improve and which should not: gains should concentrate in dimensions with well-conditioned carriers and should be absent in the weakly-explored grasp-shaping dimensions. If gains instead appear uniformly across dimensions, your mechanism story is wrong and something else is doing the work, so this is a falsification test rather than a presentation choice. It costs nothing beyond bookkeeping you should already have.

On ordering, the effective-dimension computation and the per-DoF breakdown are zero-GPU and gate everything else, so they belong in the same batch as the three-line oracle diagnostic. The snippet-selection experiment is the next cheapest and has the highest chance of becoming a real contribution. The separability claim is the argument that justifies making H1 your primary dataset, and it should be written before you run more training so that it stands as a prediction. The subspace-embedding work should wait until the canonical-frame feasibility gate is cleared, since if no shared subspace survives inspection there is nothing to build.
